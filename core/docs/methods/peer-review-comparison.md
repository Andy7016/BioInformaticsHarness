# Compare peer review and revision

Use this guide to study how a peer review concerns a paper, how the authors respond, and which changes appear in a later manuscript or code release. Keep the manuscript version visible throughout; a response and a code change answer different questions.

## A practical sequence

1. **Choose the material.** Record the submitted manuscript, review reports, author response, decision letter, later manuscript, and relevant code or data versions. Note which pages, sections, or files you actually read. Preserve reviewer anonymity and cite or paraphrase each source accurately.
2. **Review the earlier manuscript.** If you want to assess what could have been identified early, read the exact submitted version first and freeze your notes before opening the later reviews and revisions. Record each concern with the manuscript passage and the evidence needed to assess it.
3. **Map the public review.** Link each reviewer concern to the relevant research question, method choice, measurement, or reporting obligation. One concern may connect to several obligations, and one obligation may be raised by several reviewers.
4. **Follow the response.** Record the author's response, the revised passage or code, and any analysis the authors report. Keep those observations separate from a new reviewer judgment.
5. **Write the result.** State which concerns were addressed, partly addressed, left open, or could not be assessed from the available materials. A later change is evidence of a change; its effect on scientific conclusions depends on the analysis and results.

## Compare in both directions

The forward comparison asks which Harness methods or reporting obligations were raised by reviewers and authors. The reverse comparison asks which applicable obligations were not mentioned in the review. Define the eligible concerns and obligations before calculating coverage, and report the denominator and handling of overlaps. A reviewer may focus on a different question; absence from the review is a prompt for analysis, not evidence of a defect by itself.

For an early-review exercise, distinguish a retrospective test from a historically prospective review. Reading an exact submitted manuscript before seeing the review supports an observation about that exercise. A claim about what was available before publication also requires the review to have occurred before publication. When dates, versions, or access order are unknown, leave the timing result unknown.

## Sources and attribution

Use source wording and anonymous labels from the material. Mark paraphrase, quotation, and reviewer inference separately. Record publication dates, versions, locations, licenses, and the parts read. Follow each source's quotation limit and retain the original source for any claim that depends on exact wording.

## Decisions and handoff

Use `addressed`, `partly_addressed`, `open`, `unknown`, or `not_applicable` to describe each concern. Explain the evidence for each decision and identify who should act next. When a source version or required analysis is unavailable, keep that part unknown; do not fill it from a later paper or a related implementation.
